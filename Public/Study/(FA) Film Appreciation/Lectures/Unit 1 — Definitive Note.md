## 1. What is Cinema — the definition

- **Etymology**: Greek *kinema* (movement) + *graphien* (to record); historically a shortening of the French *cinématographe*.
- **One-line definition**: cinema is the *recording of movement* — the intersection of **art and technology**.
- **Composite art form**: it includes making (production), editing, processing, **distribution and presentation**.
- **Medium**: combines **visual, auditory, narrative and emotional** elements.
- Equivalent names: movie, picture, feature, talkie, flick.

| Sense of "cinema" | Means |
| --- | --- |
| Venue | The movie theatre |
| Art form | Film as an artistic medium |
| Cultural practice | Cinema as a shared cultural activity |
| Social institution | The industry, its institutions and audiences |

- Cinema **mediates our experience of the world**; it is not reality.
- It possesses a **cinematic language** — a syntax and grammar — which films reiterate, depart from and extend.
- Christian Metz: "Films are hard to explain because they are so easy to understand."

### The five axes of comparison (any two works)

| Axis | What it compares |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | Why the work exists (art, entertainment, persuasion, commerce) |
| Length | Feature vs short vs reel |
| Platform | Theatre, TV, OTT, social media |
| Formality | Register and production polish |
| Narrative | How (and whether) a story is told |

## 2. Birth of Cinema — timeline of stable facts

| Year | Event | People | Nature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1827 | **Camera obscura** | — | Projects an image |
| 1839 | **Daguerreotype** | Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre | Prints an image onto a copper sheet |
| 1872 | **Zoopraxoscope** | Eadweard Muybridge | Shows images in quick succession |
| 1890s | **Chronophotographic gun** | Étienne-Jules Marey | Captures moving images; *Venus in Motion* by Pierre Janssen |
| 1891 | **Kinetoscope / Vitascope** | Thomas Edison | Peep-hole viewer, single viewer at a time |
| 1895 | **Cinématographe** | Auguste & Louis Lumière | Captures **and** projects — a single portable machine |

- **Film strip**: coated in light-sensitive emulsion; retains an image after a short burst of light; segmented into a series of photographs.
- **Pre-cinema**: the period before the motion picture.
- **First public screening**: December 1895, *Le Salon Indien* — the basement of the Grand Café, Paris; 33 spectators; ten short films.
- **First screening in India**: 7 July 1896, **Watson's Hotel, Bombay**.
- **Actuality films** (~1,500 by 1905): *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*, *Baby's Breakfast* — documentary-style records of real life.
- **Georges Méliès**: turned cinema from record to story — the first narrative filmmaker (*A Trip to the Moon*, 1902); introduced stop-motion and dissolves.

### The two perceptual mechanisms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Persistence of vision** | The optical phenomenon by which the brain retains an image briefly after it disappears (the "after-image"); rapid succession of images (24 fps) produces the illusion of motion. |
| **Phi phenomenon** | The brain perceives *movement between* static images shown in quick sequence (two alternately blinking dots read as one moving dot); the psychological foundation of motion in cinema and animation. |

## 3. Cinema and the Other Arts

- The ancients recognised seven arts: History, Poetry, Comedy, Tragedy, Music, Dance, Astronomy; poetry split into **Lyric**, **Dramatic**, **Epic** → the modern literary arts.
- **Photography** developed closer to the sciences than to art — it is **mimesis** (imitation/representation).
- Film is a **medium of communication**: it mediates experience.

| Art | Definitive relationship to film |
| --- | --- |
| Literature | Film adapts (converts) narrative, setting and description from word to image; adaptation is **translation between languages and mediums**. |
| Theatre | Both tell stories with runtime and performance; theatre communicates through **voice**, film through **expressions**; the evolution ran scene → act → back to scene (realism). |
| Architecture | Buildings and design constitute the setting; iconic architecture functions as a significant element of meaning. |
| Music | Enhances scenes, provokes emotion, supplies rhythm, continuity and flow; iconic soundtracks enter popular culture. |
| Dance | Musicals integrate dance, music and acting to tell the story. |

- **Conclusion of the unit**: film **borrows** (adaptation — not adoption), works **non-linearly / non-sequentially** when needed, and its borrowing uses the specific language of the **semiotics of filmmaking**.

## 4. Narrative, Character and the Auteur

### Narrative components

| Element | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Plot | The arrangement of events; conventionally the three-act structure |
| Character | Agents of the plot (see types in [[Unit 1 — Definitive Note#Characters|Characters]]) |
| Setting | Environment, social conditions, time period, location, weather — **backdrop** (passive) or **integral** (governs the characters) |
| Theme | The central idea — love, death, betrayal, patriotism |
| Point of view | Through whose eyes the story is told |

### Three-act structure

| Act | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Act 1 — Setup | Hook, setting, introduction of the problem/conflict |
| Act 2 — Confrontation | Rising action; the protagonist's struggle with the conflict |
| Act 3 — Resolution | Climax, then resolution of the problem/conflict, then conclusion |

### Characters

- Revealed through **dialogue**, **action**, **relationships**, **visuals** (costumes, makeup, hair) and **backstory**.
- Type pairs: protagonist/antagonist; major/minor; **dynamic** (changes) / **static** (unchanged); **round** (complex) / **flat–stock** (stereotyped).

### Auteur theory

- The **director is the author** of a film.
- Though many teams collaborate, the director imposes a **unified, coherent vision**.
- The director's personal sensibility is **identifiable across their work**.
- Celebrated artistry at the heart of the studio system; championed by *Cahiers du Cinéma* and the French New Wave.

## 5. 20th Century Film Movements

- Avant-garde roots: **Dadaism (1916)** — anti-art after WWI; **Surrealism (1924, André Breton)** — the unconscious and irrational (*Un Chien Andalou*).

| Movement | Period | Definitive features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| German Expressionism | c. 1910–1920s | Response to collective anxiety after WWI; high angles, deep shadows (**chiaroscuro**), extreme tilts, **impossible sets**; landmark: *The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari* (1920) |
| Italian Neo-Realism | late 1940s–early 1950s | Post-WWII; used surviving infrastructure after studios were destroyed; gazes at poverty and unemployment; realistic, no studios, modest budgets, **rawness** |
| Classical Hollywood | 1930s–1960s | **Big 5** (MGM, Paramount, Fox, Warner Bros., RKO); vertical integration of production–casting–development–distribution; stars, sound, Technicolor; ended by TV, blacklisting, anti-trust |
| French New Wave | 1950s–1960s | Opposed Hollywood's studio system; **Auteur theory**; jump cuts, location shooting, handheld cameras, long takes, direct sound, available light, breaking the fourth wall |

---

## 6. Film and Society

- Films are **not made in a vacuum** — they must be understood in context.
- A film may serve **art for art's sake** or engagement with **social systems** (or both).
- Representative Indian films (2015+): *Manjhi*, *Fandry*, *Masaan*, *Pink*, *Jai Bhim*, *Kaala*, *Article 15*, *Sairat*, *Thappad*.
- Cinema also **influences society**: it can change perceptions and encode ideology.

## Glossary

> [!tip] Objective, fixed definitions — these do not change across revisions, films or sources.

- **Actuality** — a short documentary-style film recording real life (e.g. *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*).
- **Affect theory** — the study of how film engages the *body* in emotion; feelings are as significant as narrative understanding.
- **Auteur theory** — the doctrine that the director is the author of a film, identifiable by a consistent personal vision across works.
- **Backlight** — light placed behind the subject, separating it from the background in three-point lighting.
- **Blocking** — the choreography of actors' movement and their engagement with the environment.
- **Camera angle** — the position of the camera relative to the subject: **low** (subject dominates), **high** (subject diminished), **eye-level** (neutral), **dutch** (tilted horizon; unease), **over-the-shoulder** (conversation), **shoulder-level**, **overhead** (god's-eye).
- **Camera movement** — **static** (none), **pan/whip pan** (horizontal rotation), **tilt** (vertical), **push in / pull out** (approach/withdraw), **roll** (axial rotation; disorientation), **zoom** (lens movement only), **arc** (camera circles subject), **tracking** (camera physically travels with the subject over time).
- **Character** — a participant in the narrative; types: protagonist, antagonist, major, minor, dynamic, static, round, flat/stock.
- **Chiaroscuro** — strong contrast between light and dark in an image; signature of German Expressionism.
- **Cinematography** — the art of capturing images for film: camera movement, lighting techniques, composition, transitions, editing.
- **Cinématographe** — the Lumière brothers' 1895 camera-projector; the device that established cinema as public spectacle.
- **Continuity editing** — cutting that maintains seamless, invisible space and time.
- **Cross cutting** — alternating between two or more lines of action in different locations.
- **Cutaway** — a brief insert of related detail cut away from, then back to, the main action.
- **Dadaism** — 1916 anti-art movement rejecting reason after WWI; parent of surrealism.
- **Daguerreotype** — 1839 photographic process printing an image onto a copper sheet (Daguerre).
- **Diegetic sound** — sound that exists inside the film's world; the characters can hear it (dialogue, footsteps, an on-screen radio).
- **Dissolve** — a transition in which one shot fades into the next; marks passage of time.
- **Dutch angle** — camera tilted so the horizon is skewed; signals unease or disorientation.
- **Dynamic character** — a character who changes through the story.
- **Editing** — the selection and arrangement of shots into a sequence.
- **Eye-level angle** — camera at the subject's height; neutral, natural perspective.
- **Fade** — transition to or from black/colour; opens or closes a segment.
- **Film strip** — celluloid coated in light-sensitive emulsion that retains images after exposure; segmented into still photographs.
- **Film language** — the syntax and grammar of cinema: shot, scene, sequence, editing, mise-en-scène.
- **Fourth wall** — the imaginary barrier between audience and actors; breaking it addresses the audience directly.
- **Frame** — a single still image projected on screen; one instant in time.
- **Genre** — a category of films sharing narrative/stylistic conventions; determines expectations of characters, setting, plot, structure, tone.
- **Genre codes** — the established relationships between signs and signifiers (costume, music, dialogue delivery) that group films into genres.
- **High-angle shot** — camera above the subject; subject appears small, vulnerable.
- **High-key lighting** — even, bright, shadowless illumination; comedies, optimism.
- **Icon (semiotics)** — a sign resembling its referent (smoke → fire).
- **Index (semiotics)** — a sign pointing to something else through a causal or direct link; show, don't tell.
- **Integral setting** — a setting that controls/creates the characters (e.g. New York).
- **J & L cut** — a cut where audio leads (J) or lags (L) the picture; smooths transitions.
- **Jump cut** — an abrupt cut within the same shot; discontinuity; French New Wave signature.
- **Key light** — the main illumination of the subject in three-point lighting.
- **Kinetoscope** — Edison's 1891 peep-hole motion-picture viewer.
- **Low-angle shot** — camera below the subject; subject appears powerful, dominant.
- **Low-key lighting** — strong shadows, high contrast; noir, dread, secrecy.
- **Mimesis** — representation/imitation of reality; the photographic and cinematic impulse.
- **Mise-en-scène** — everything placed in front of the camera (actors, scenery, props, lighting); the arrangement *within* the scene; popularised for cinema by André Bazin.
- **Montage** — (1) the rapid sequencing of shots to compress time/emotion; (2) editing in general (Bazin opposed montage to mise-en-scène).
- **A montage sequence** — a series of shots edited together to condense events.
- **Neo-Realism (Italian)** — post-WWII movement using available infrastructure to depict social reality with raw, modest means.
- **New Wave (French)** — 1950s–60s movement against Hollywood's studio system; auteur theory, jump cuts, location shooting, handheld cameras, long takes, direct sound, available light.
- **Non-diegetic sound** — sound outside the film's world; only the audience hears it (score, narration).
- **Overhead shot** — camera directly above; god's-eye perspective; scale, isolation.
- **Over-the-shoulder shot** — camera behind one character's shoulder viewing another; conversation framing.
- **Pan** — horizontal camera rotation; reveals space or follows movement; whip pan adds energy.
- **Persistence of vision** — the retention of an image on the retina/brain briefly after viewing; basis of perceived motion at 24 fps.
- **Phi phenomenon** — perceived movement between static images shown in quick sequence; psychological foundation of cinema and animation.
- **Production design** — the creation of a film's visual world: set, colour.
- **Protagonist / Antagonist** — central character / opponent.
- **Push in / Pull out** — camera physically advances toward or retreats from the subject.
- **REPETITION and DIFFERENCE** — Steve Neale's formula for how genres transmit codes between films.
- **Resolution** — the act of the three-act structure in which the conflict is solved.
- **Scene** — a series of connected shots forming one episode; usually one location and event.
- **Score** — music composed for the film (contrast: soundtrack).
- **Sequence** — a series of related scenes forming a distinct narrative unit.
- **Setting** — the environment of the narrative: time period, location, social conditions, weather.
- **Shot** — a continuous, unbroken run of frames from one camera.
- **Signifier / Signified** — the form of a sign / the concept it evokes; together they form the sign.
- **Soundtrack** — the assembled music of a film, including pre-existing songs.
- **Static camera** — no movement; non-distracting, deliberate.
- **Studio system** — Hollywood's vertical integration of production, distribution and exhibition through the Big 5.
- **Symbol (semiotics)** — a sign whose meaning rests on arbitrary cultural convention.
- **Theme** — the central idea of a narrative (love, death, betrayal, patriotism).
- **Three-act structure** — setup (Act 1) → confrontation (Act 2) → resolution (Act 3).
- **Three-point lighting** — key + fill + backlight; the standard legible illumination setup.
- **Tilt** — vertical camera rotation; verticality, awe, spectacle.
- **Tracking shot** — the camera physically moves with the subject over an extended period.
- **World cinema** — films produced outside the dominant (Hollywood) system; promoted at festivals such as Cannes and Sundance.
- **Zoopraxoscope** — Muybridge's 1872 device projecting images in quick succession.
