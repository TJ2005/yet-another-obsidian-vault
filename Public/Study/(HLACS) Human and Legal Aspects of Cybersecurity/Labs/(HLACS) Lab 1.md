# Theory

The Information Technology Act, 2000, commonly called the **IT Act, 2000**, is India’s principal legislation dealing with electronic records, electronic signatures, electronic commerce, cyber offences and certain forms of intermediary liability.

The Act received presidential assent on **9 June 2000**. Its original purpose was to provide legal recognition to electronic transactions conducted through electronic data interchange and other forms of electronic communication. It also enabled electronic filing with government agencies and amended related laws so that electronic records could be recognised in legal and commercial proceedings.

The IT Act also has limited extraterritorial application. Under Section 75, conduct committed outside India may be covered where the offence or contravention involves a computer, computer system or computer network located in India.

The Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008 substantially expanded the original legislation. It introduced or revised provisions relating to:

* Electronic signatures
* Identity theft
* Cheating by personation
* Violation of privacy
* Cyberterrorism
* Obscene and sexually explicit online content
* Child sexual abuse material
* Data protection and reasonable security practices
* Intermediary liability
* Government interception and blocking powers
* Protected systems and critical information infrastructure
* CERT-In

The amendment was enacted in 2008 and its principal provisions came into force in 2009.

The IT Act is not administered entirely by CERT-In. Different authorities exercise powers under different provisions. CERT-In is specifically established as the national agency for cyber incident response under **Section 70B**.

# Review Questions

## 1. Explain the need for the Information Technology Act in India

The Information Technology Act was required because traditional Indian laws were originally drafted for physical documents, handwritten signatures, tangible property and conventional offences. They did not adequately address transactions and crimes occurring through computers and electronic networks.

### 1.1 Legal recognition of electronic records

Before the IT Act, legal and commercial systems largely depended on paper documents. The Act gave electronic records legal recognition and allowed information to be retained and communicated electronically.

Sections 4 and 7 deal with legal recognition and retention of electronic records.

### 1.2 Recognition of electronic and digital signatures

Electronic commerce requires a method for authenticating the identity of a person approving an electronic document.

The Act recognised digital signatures and, after the 2008 amendment, the broader concept of electronic signatures. It also created a regulatory framework for Certifying Authorities that issue electronic signature certificates.

### 1.3 Facilitation of electronic commerce

Online contracts, electronic payments, online banking, e-commerce platforms and electronic invoices require statutory recognition.

The IT Act helped establish that a contract cannot be denied legal validity merely because electronic means were used in its formation.

### 1.4 Facilitation of electronic governance

The legislation enabled government departments to:

* Accept electronic applications
* Issue licences electronically
* Receive electronic payments
* Retain records electronically
* Deliver public services through digital platforms

### 1.5 Creation of specific cyber offences

Traditional criminal laws did not clearly address acts such as:

* Unauthorised access to computer systems
* Introduction of computer viruses
* Theft of passwords
* Online impersonation
* Cyberterrorism
* Destruction of computer data
* Publishing obscene material electronically
* Breach of confidentiality
* Attacks on protected systems

The IT Act defined such conduct and prescribed penalties or compensation.

### 1.6 Protection of computer systems and data

Section 43 provides compensation-related liability for unauthorised access, copying data, introducing contaminants, damaging systems, disrupting services and denying authorised users access.

Where acts covered by Section 43 are committed dishonestly or fraudulently, Section 66 may impose criminal punishment.

### 1.7 Protection of privacy and confidentiality

The Act contains provisions dealing with:

* Unauthorised disclosure of information
* Breach of confidentiality
* Violation of bodily privacy
* Failure by organisations to protect sensitive data
* Disclosure of information in breach of a lawful contract

### 1.8 Regulation of intermediaries

Internet communications often pass through intermediaries such as:

* Internet Service Providers
* Social-media platforms
* Search engines
* Web-hosting services
* Online marketplaces
* Cloud-service providers
* Messaging platforms

Section 79 provides conditional protection from liability while requiring intermediaries to satisfy due-diligence requirements.

### 1.9 Protection of critical information infrastructure

Digital attacks on power grids, banking systems, defence networks, telecommunications and government infrastructure can affect national security.

The 2008 amendment strengthened provisions relating to protected systems, critical information infrastructure, cyberterrorism and incident response.

### 1.10 Extraterritorial cybercrime

Cybercrime may be committed from outside India while targeting systems located within India. Section 75 allows the Act to apply to certain conduct outside India when an Indian computer resource is involved.

# 3. What was Section 66A? Was it unconstitutional?

## Meaning of Section 66A

Section 66A was inserted through the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008.

It criminalised sending information through a computer resource or communication device where the information was described using expressions such as:

* Grossly offensive
* Menacing
* Known to be false and sent for causing annoyance
* Inconvenience
* Danger
* Obstruction
* Insult
* Injury
* Criminal intimidation
* Enmity
* Hatred
* Ill will

It also covered certain misleading electronic messages and messages intended to deceive recipients about their origin.

The punishment could extend to imprisonment for three years along with a fine.

## Constitutional challenge

Section 66A was challenged before the Supreme Court in **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India**.

On **24 March 2015**, the Supreme Court struck down Section 66A in its entirety. The Court held that it violated the freedom of speech and expression guaranteed by Article 19(1)(a) and was not saved by the reasonable restrictions permitted under Article 19(2).

## Why Section 66A was unconstitutional

### 3.1 Vague terminology

Terms such as “annoyance,” “inconvenience,” “grossly offensive” and “ill will” were not defined with sufficient precision.

A person could not reliably determine what speech was prohibited.

### 3.2 Excessively broad scope

The section could apply to legitimate:

* Political criticism
* Satire
* Journalism
* Academic discussion
* Social commentary
* Harsh but lawful opinions
* Public disagreement

### 3.3 Chilling effect on speech

People might avoid lawful expression because they feared arrest or prosecution. This discouragement of lawful speech is described as a chilling effect.

### 3.4 Weak connection with Article 19(2)

Restrictions on speech must fall within constitutionally recognised grounds such as:

* Security of the State
* Public order
* Decency or morality
* Defamation
* Incitement to an offence

Section 66A used much wider concepts, including annoyance and inconvenience, which were not by themselves grounds listed in Article 19(2).

### 3.5 Arbitrary enforcement

Because the wording was subjective, different police officers could interpret the same communication differently, creating a risk of arbitrary arrests.

## Conclusion regarding Section 66A

Yes, Section 66A was unconstitutional because its vague and overbroad wording imposed a disproportionate restriction on online expression.

The provision is no longer legally enforceable. The current official consolidated text records Section 66A as omitted.

However, striking down Section 66A did not legalise all harmful online conduct. Threats, stalking, defamation, obscenity, cheating, impersonation, incitement and other unlawful acts may still be prosecuted under other applicable laws.

# 4. Applicable Sections for the Given Scenarios

## A. Cyberterrorism

### Primary section: Section 66F

Section 66F applies where a person, with the required intention relating to national security or terror, uses computer resources in ways such as:

* Denying access to an authorised person
* Attempting to penetrate or access a computer resource without authorisation
* Introducing a computer contaminant
* Causing or being likely to cause death, injury or damage to property
* Disrupting essential supplies or services
* Adversely affecting critical information infrastructure
* Accessing restricted information likely to harm national security or foreign relations

Cyberterrorism may be punished with imprisonment which may extend to imprisonment for life.

### Other potentially applicable sections

* Section 43: unauthorised access, disruption or introduction of contaminants
* Section 66: dishonest or fraudulent computer-related offences
* Section 70: unauthorised access to a protected system
* Section 121 or other applicable provisions of general criminal law where national security is involved

### Answer

**Applicable section: Section 66F, potentially read with Sections 43, 66 and 70.**

---

## B. Distribution of Malware

“Malware” may include viruses, worms, ransomware, spyware, trojans and other malicious code.

### Section 43(c)

Section 43(c) applies when a person introduces or causes the introduction of a computer contaminant or computer virus into a computer, system or network without permission.

### Section 43(i)

Section 43(i) may apply where information residing in a computer resource is destroyed, deleted or altered, or where its value or utility is diminished.

### Section 66

If an act listed under Section 43 is performed **dishonestly or fraudulently**, it becomes a criminal offence under Section 66.

### Section 65

Section 65 may apply where malware is used to knowingly conceal, destroy or alter computer source code that is legally required to be maintained.

### Section 66F

If the malware operation satisfies the special national-security requirements of cyberterrorism, Section 66F may apply.

### Answer

**Applicable sections: Sections 43(c), 43(i) and 66. Depending on the facts, Sections 65 and 66F may also apply.**

Merely possessing or studying malware for authorised research does not automatically establish the offence. The prosecution must establish the prohibited act and the required intention.

---

## C. Hacking Government Websites

“Hacking” is not limited to one section. The applicable section depends on the system targeted and the attacker’s intention.

### Section 43

This applies to conduct such as:

* Unauthorised access
* Copying or extracting data
* Introducing malware
* Damaging a website
* Disrupting services
* Denying access to authorised users

### Section 66

If the Section 43 acts are performed dishonestly or fraudulently, Section 66 applies criminally.

### Section 65

If the attacker alters or destroys computer source code, Section 65 may apply.

### Section 70

If the government website or underlying system has formally been declared a **protected system**, unauthorised access may attract Section 70.

A government website is not automatically a protected system merely because it belongs to the government; the relevant statutory designation and facts must be verified.

### Section 66F

If the attack is intended to threaten India’s unity, integrity, security or sovereignty, strike terror, disrupt essential services or affect critical information infrastructure, Section 66F may apply.

### Answer

**Applicable sections: Sections 43 and 66. Sections 65, 70 and 66F may additionally apply depending on the nature of the system and the attacker’s intention.**

---

## D. Distribution of Obscene Material

### Section 67

Section 67 punishes publishing or transmitting material in electronic form that is:

* Lascivious
* Appeals to prurient interests
* Tends to deprave and corrupt persons likely to see, read or hear it

### Section 67A

Where the material contains a sexually explicit act or conduct, the more specific Section 67A may apply.

### Answer

**Applicable section: Section 67. If the content depicts sexually explicit acts or conduct, Section 67A applies.**

---

## E. Child Pornography

The modern and more appropriate expression is **child sexual abuse material**, or CSAM, because the material records or facilitates sexual abuse and exploitation of children.

### Primary section: Section 67B

Section 67B covers conduct involving electronic material depicting children in sexually explicit acts or conduct, including specified acts of:

* Publishing
* Transmitting
* Creating
* Collecting
* Seeking
* Browsing
* Downloading
* Advertising
* Promoting
* Exchanging
* Distributing

It also addresses certain forms of online enticement and facilitation involving children.

### Other applicable laws

Depending on the facts, provisions of the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 and general criminal law may also apply.

### Answer

**Applicable section: Section 67B of the IT Act, along with relevant provisions of the POCSO Act where applicable.**

---

## F. Launching a DoS or DDoS Attack Against a Corporate Website

A **Denial-of-Service attack** attempts to make a system or service unavailable to legitimate users. A **Distributed Denial-of-Service attack** uses multiple systems or devices to generate the attack.

### Section 43(f)

Section 43(f) specifically addresses denying or causing denial of access to an authorised person.

### Section 43(e)

This may apply where the attack disrupts a computer system or network.

### Section 43(c)

This may apply if malware, botnet agents or other computer contaminants are introduced.

### Section 66

Where these Section 43 acts are committed dishonestly or fraudulently, Section 66 creates criminal liability.

### Section 66F

A DDoS attack does not automatically amount to cyberterrorism. Section 66F applies only where the attack satisfies its additional national-security, terror or critical-infrastructure requirements.

### Answer

**Applicable sections: Sections 43(e), 43(f) and 66. Section 43(c) may apply when malware or botnets are used. Section 66F applies only in exceptional cyberterrorism circumstances.**

# 5. Explain the Liabilities of Intermediaries Such as ISPs

## Meaning of an intermediary

Under the IT Act, an intermediary is a person who, on behalf of another person, receives, stores or transmits an electronic record or provides a service relating to that record.

The definition covers various entities, including:

* Internet Service Providers
* Telecom service providers
* Network service providers
* Web-hosting providers
* Search engines
* Online marketplaces
* Online payment sites
* Cybercafés
* Social-media platforms
* Cloud-service providers

## Section 79: Conditional safe-harbour protection

Section 79 provides intermediaries with conditional immunity from liability for third-party information, data or communication links hosted or transmitted through them.

This protection is known as **safe harbour**.

An intermediary is not automatically responsible for everything that users send, store or publish through its service.

However, the protection is conditional.

## Conditions for safe harbour

### 5.1 Limited role

The intermediary’s function should generally be limited to:

* Providing access to a communication system
* Transmitting information
* Temporarily storing information
* Hosting third-party information

### 5.2 No initiation of transmission

The intermediary should not initiate the unlawful transmission.

### 5.3 No selection of receiver

The intermediary should not select the receiver of the transmission in a manner that makes it responsible for the content.

### 5.4 No selection or modification of information

The intermediary should not select or materially modify the transmitted information, subject to the technical functioning of its service.

### 5.5 Due diligence

The intermediary must observe due diligence while performing its duties and comply with applicable rules and government requirements.

### 5.6 No conspiracy, abetment or assistance

Safe harbour is unavailable where the intermediary has:

* Conspired in the unlawful act
* Abetted it
* Aided it
* Induced it

### 5.7 Compliance with lawful orders

The intermediary must respond appropriately to valid legal directions and court or government orders.

In **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India**, the Supreme Court read down the relevant part of Section 79 so that takedown obligations are linked to a court order or a valid government notification concerning unlawful material under Article 19(2), rather than requiring intermediaries to independently decide every private allegation of illegality.

## Other statutory responsibilities

### Section 67C: Preservation of information

Intermediaries must preserve and retain information for the prescribed duration and in the prescribed manner.

Failure to comply can result in punishment.

### Section 69: Interception, monitoring and decryption assistance

Intermediaries may be required to provide technical assistance in implementing a lawful interception, monitoring or decryption order.

Failure to provide required assistance can attract punishment under the Act.

### Section 69A: Blocking orders

Intermediaries may be required to block public access to specified information under a valid government order.

Failure to comply may attract criminal liability.

### Section 69B: Traffic-data monitoring

Intermediaries may be required to provide technical assistance for authorised monitoring and collection of traffic data for cybersecurity purposes.

### Section 70B: CERT-In directions

CERT-In may call for information and issue directions to service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates and other persons as authorised under Section 70B.

Failure to comply with lawful CERT-In directions may result in punishment. CERT-In officially identifies its directions as being issued under Section 70B of the IT Act.

## Situations in which an ISP may become liable

An ISP or other intermediary may lose safe-harbour protection where it:

1. Creates or materially contributes to unlawful content.
2. Initiates an unlawful transmission.
3. Selects the receiver or modifies the unlawful information beyond its neutral technical role.
4. Knowingly conspires in or aids an offence.
5. Ignores a valid court order or legally authorised government direction.
6. Fails to follow prescribed due diligence.
7. Fails to preserve required records.
8. Refuses to assist with lawful interception, blocking or cybersecurity directions.
9. Directly commits an offence under the IT Act.
10. Makes prohibited disclosure of user information or confidential data.

## Nature of intermediary liability

Intermediary liability is therefore not absolute.

An ISP is generally not expected to be the original publisher of every communication passing through its network. At the same time, it cannot remain protected when it actively participates in illegal conduct or fails to satisfy statutory obligations.

The legal position may be summarised as:

> **Neutral intermediary + due diligence + compliance with lawful orders = possible safe-harbour protection.**

> **Active participation, unlawful assistance or statutory non-compliance = possible loss of protection and legal liability.**

# Brief Summary of ITA 2000 and ITAA 2008

## Information Technology Act, 2000

The IT Act, 2000 created the basic legal framework for India’s digital environment. Its major contributions included:

* Recognition of electronic records
* Recognition of digital signatures
* Electronic governance
* Regulation of Certifying Authorities
* Civil liability for unauthorised computer access and damage
* Creation of computer-related offences
* Adjudication and appeals
* Limited extraterritorial application
* Initial intermediary-liability provisions

## Information Technology Amendment Act, 2008

The 2008 amendment updated the legislation to address newer technologies and cyber threats. It:

* Introduced electronic signatures
* Inserted Section 43A on failure to protect data
* Recast Section 66
* Introduced identity theft under Section 66C
* Introduced online personation under Section 66D
* Introduced violation of privacy under Section 66E
* Introduced cyberterrorism under Section 66F
* Expanded electronic-content offences through Sections 67A and 67B
* Introduced preservation duties under Section 67C
* Expanded interception, blocking and monitoring powers
* Strengthened critical-infrastructure provisions
* Established CERT-In statutorily under Section 70B
* Revised intermediary safe-harbour protection under Section 79
* Introduced the Examiner of Electronic Evidence under Section 79A

It also inserted Section 66A, but that provision was later struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015.

# Findings

1. The IT Act provides the legal foundation for electronic transactions and cybercrime regulation in India.
2. The 2008 amendment substantially expanded the Act to cover modern cyber threats.
3. Section 66A was unconstitutional because it imposed vague and excessive restrictions on online speech.
4. The same cyber incident may attract multiple sections depending on the attacker’s intention, method and target.
5. Section 43 generally addresses unauthorised acts and compensation, while Section 66 criminalises Section 43 acts committed dishonestly or fraudulently.
6. Cyberterrorism requires more than ordinary hacking; the requirements of Section 66F must be satisfied.
7. Intermediaries receive only conditional safe-harbour protection.
8. CERT-In’s statutory role arises under Section 70B; it does not administer the entire IT Act.
9. Metadata, logs, electronic records and other digital evidence are important for proving offences under the Act.
10. Legal classification must be based on the complete facts and required mental element, not merely the name commonly given to the cyberattack.

# Conclusion

The Information Technology Act, 2000 is the principal legal framework governing electronic records, electronic commerce and several categories of cyber offences in India.

The Information Technology Amendment Act, 2008 modernised this framework by introducing provisions concerning identity theft, online impersonation, privacy violations, cyberterrorism, child sexual abuse material, critical information infrastructure, intermediary liability and incident response.

The experiment demonstrates that cyber incidents cannot always be classified under a single section. Correct legal analysis requires examination of:

* The act performed
* The system targeted
* The damage caused
* Whether authorisation existed
* Whether the act was dishonest or fraudulent
* Whether national security or critical infrastructure was affected
* Whether the accused was an intermediary or active participant

Thus, the IT Act remains an important foundation of Indian cyber law, although it operates alongside other criminal, evidentiary, data-protection and sector-specific laws.
